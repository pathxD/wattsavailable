How it works

How the money flows

These incentives exist because saving a kilowatt-hour is cheaper than generating one. Your utility gets reimbursed for helping you use less — everyone’s bill benefits.

01

You (or your contractor) contact your utility

Before any work starts, you ask your utility’s energy-efficiency or energy-advisor team about your project, citing the measures you found here. They confirm what they offer and start the paperwork. Contractors often handle this step for customers.

02

The project gets done and verified

Once the paperwork is approved, you do the project. The utility verifies the installation — some measures need certified installers or specific documentation, which the catalog notes.

03

BPA reimburses the utility, the utility pays you

BPA pays your utility the catalog amount for verified savings. Your utility pays its rebate to you (or to your contractor, who may pass it through as a discount). BPA never pays end customers directly.

The ground rules

Three things to know before you start

Paperwork first, project second

To receive an incentive, your utility must approve your application before the project begins. Work that has already started or finished generally can’t be incentivized. This is the single most common way customers lose money they qualified for.

Your utility makes the final call

The UES catalog is a menu, not a mandate. Utilities choose which measures to offer and set their own customer-facing rebate amounts — sometimes lower than the BPA reference payment, sometimes higher, and sometimes through their own separate programs. Treat figures here as a conversation starter.

Deemed vs. custom projects

The measures listed here are “deemed” — pre-calculated savings and payments for standard equipment. Bigger or unusual projects can run as custom projects instead, where incentives are calculated from your project’s actual engineering. When both could apply, your utility decides which path fits. FAQ

Common questions

What is BPA, and what is a “UES measure”?

The Bonneville Power Administration is a federal agency that markets power from the Columbia River hydro system to Northwest utilities. To keep demand in check, it funds energy-efficiency work through those utilities. A UES (Unit Energy Savings) measure is a pre-approved efficiency upgrade — say, R-49 attic insulation in an electrically heated home — with agreed savings and an agreed payment per unit, published in BPA’s Implementation Manual and UES Measures List.

Is the dollar amount shown what I’ll get?

Not exactly. It’s what BPA reimburses your utility per unit installed. Your utility sets the customer rebate — often similar, sometimes different, occasionally zero if they don’t offer that measure. That’s why the last step here is contacting them before you build anything.

Why do payments depend on “heating zones”?

A heat pump or attic insulation saves more electricity in Spokane than in Seattle, because winters are colder. BPA divides the region into three heating zones by climate severity; savings and payments scale with them.

Do I apply, or does my contractor?

Either. Many insulation, window and heat-pump contractors handle the utility paperwork routinely and pass the incentive through as a discount on your invoice. If a contractor claims an incentive on your behalf, ask to see the amount on the paperwork.

My electricity comes from a utility that isn’t a BPA customer. Is this useless to me?

Not useless — but the amounts won’t apply. Investor-owned utilities (Puget Sound Energy, PGE, Avista, Idaho Power, NorthWestern) run their own rebate programs, frequently covering the same equipment. The project list you build here is still a good checklist to bring them.

What are the income-qualified amounts?

Many measures carry substantially higher payments (e.g., up to $6,000 for a ductless heat pump) for income-qualified or low-income households, usually capped at actual project cost. Each utility manages its own qualification process, often with community-action agencies. If money is tight, always ask about these first — some cover nearly the entire project.

How current is this data?

This site uses the BPA UES Measures List effective April 1, 2026, filtered to currently active measures with a published payment. The catalog updates every April and October; always confirm current figures with your utility or the official BPA page.
